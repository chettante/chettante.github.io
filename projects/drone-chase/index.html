Drone Chase — Finding and Tracking a Flying Target

A follower quadrotor that finds, follows and re-acquires a leader drone using only what its camera can see, in a GenoM3 and Gazebo simulation

Two quadrotors share the sky. The leader flies its own trajectory; the follower has to find it, stay close behind it and, when it slips out of view, find it again. The catch: the follower may only use what an on-board camera would see. Everything runs in simulation, on the same flight software used on real aerial robots: Gazebo for the physics, the telekyb3 flight stack (GenoM3 components from LAAS-CNRS) for each drone, and our Python mission code on top.

I designed and wrote the follower’s perception and control: the camera model, the estimation of the leader’s velocity, the tracking controller, the search strategy and their integration in the state machine.

How it is built

Each drone runs its own copy of the telekyb3 stack. rotorcraft talks to the motors and the IMU, pom is a Kalman filter that fuses the IMU with motion-capture poses, nhfc is the flight controller and maneuver turns our commands into smooth, dynamically feasible references for it. The launcher starts two instances of every component (_1 for the leader, _2 for the follower) and one shared optitrack.

On top of them, main_statemachine.py runs a 20 Hz loop through python-genomix: it moves the leader along its trajectory, simulates the follower’s camera, steps the state machine and sends the resulting velocity command to the follower’s maneuver. All parameters live in a single config.py, and a small script copies the field of view into the Gazebo camera sensor, so the image rendered by Gazebo and the camera model in the code always match.

A camera without image processing

The leader’s position comes from motion capture, but the follower is allowed to use it only while the leader is inside the camera’s field of view: a pyramid of 87° × 58°, from 0.1 to 10 m, mounted 45° off the drone’s first arm. This turns perfect data into the realistic problem: the leader can be lost, and the follower has to keep it in the picture.

The camera is rigid, as in Gazebo. To accelerate, a quadrotor has to tilt, and every degree of tilt moves the leader by one degree in the image. So the tracking acceleration is a tilt budget: a = g · tan(10°) ≈ 1.7 m/s².

Estimating the leader’s velocity. The camera only gives positions. The velocity comes from differentiating the leader’s position between frames, computed in the world frame (the camera frame rotates with the drone), restarted every time the leader re-enters the image and frozen when motion capture has not refreshed yet, which would otherwise make the estimate jump between 0 and twice the real value.

Four states

  • IDLE — hover and look. If the leader appears, start tracking; after 5 s without it, explore.
  • EXPLORING — one lap on a ring around the search volume, with the camera always facing its centre.
  • TRACKING — follow the leader and keep it centred. If it leaves the image, keep following its extrapolated position for up to 2 s before giving up.
  • SEARCHING — fly to where the leader should be now, then scan the area; back to tracking as soon as it is seen again.

Each state is a small class whose update() returns the next state, so the transitions stay explicit and every state can be developed and tested on its own.

Tracking: P + feedforward

The follower aims for a point 3 m behind the leader along the line that joins them, at the leader’s altitude. The command is the leader’s estimated velocity (feedforward) plus a proportional correction toward that point, and the yaw keeps the leader on the camera axis using both the angular error and the rate at which the bearing is turning.

Two rules keep the leader in the picture. Near the side edges of the image the drone rotates first and corrects its position later; near the top or bottom edge it stops approaching, because getting closer increases the vertical angle. A rate limiter enforces the tilt budget, and when tracking starts the limiter begins from the follower’s measured velocity instead of zero (bumpless transfer), so switching state never makes the drone jerk.

Losing the leader, and finding it again

Searching starts where the leader should be by now: its last position plus its last velocity times the time since it was lost. In a single smooth move the follower turns to put that point at the centre of the image, gets within 3 m of it and matches its altitude. If the leader is still not in view, a fixed scan follows: hold, fly home, climb while spinning, descend while spinning.

Exploring is for when the leader has not been seen at all: a straight connection to a ring that encloses the 20 × 20 m volume, then one lap at 10 m/s, rising from 6 to 14 m and back, with the camera pointed at the centre. Point-to-point moves use a quintic (smoothstep) time law sized on the speed and acceleration limits, so they start and stop with zero velocity and acceleration.

The leader

The leader flies a figure-8 that climbs from 5 to 10 m and back. It is re-parametrised by arc length, so the speed stays constant along the curves after a smooth start, and the whole trajectory is computed once before the loop and then streamed to maneuver one sample per tick. For harder tests the leader can instead fly through 100 random waypoints spread over a 200 × 200 m area, at speeds of tens of metres per second.

What I learned

  • The planner has limits, and they must be respected upstream. Rate-limiting every command and never sending a non-finite value avoids the planner rejecting or crashing on a sudden change.
  • Switching states is part of the controller. Bumpless transfer and a short grace period stop the drone from jerking at every transition and from giving up on a leader that is only briefly out of view.
  • Perception constraints shape the control. With a rigid camera, the field of view decides how hard the follower may accelerate and in which order it should correct yaw and position.
  • One configuration file for everything. Keeping all gains, limits and the camera geometry in one place, and generating the Gazebo camera from it, avoids mismatches between the code and the simulator.
