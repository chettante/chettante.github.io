AI-ARMS is a team of the Automation Engineering Association (AEA) at Politecnico di Milano working on low-cost robot manipulation. We built an SO-101 arm from scratch, made it work under leader–follower teleoperation, and then used the demonstrations to teach it a task on its own.

My part was the last step: training an imitation-learning policy on the recorded demonstrations and running it on the real arm, so that it performs pick-and-place autonomously, with no teleoperation.

The SO-101 arm

The SO-101 is an open-source, 3D-printable robot arm designed by TheRobotStudio together with Hugging Face. It is the successor of the SO-100, with easier assembly and better wiring, and it has become one of the most popular platforms for learning-based manipulation because:

  • every structural part is printed in PLA, and the design files are released under Apache-2.0;
  • the parts for a full leader + follower pair cost a few hundred euros;
  • it is natively supported by LeRobot, Hugging Face’s library for real-world robot learning.

A setup has two arms. The follower does the work; the leader, with a handle and trigger, is moved by hand to drive the follower. Both use Feetech STS3215 serial-bus servos, with different gear ratios on the leader to make it easy to back-drive.

Build and teleoperation

We printed and assembled both arms, then brought them up with LeRobot’s command-line tools:

  1. Motor setup — assign an ID to every servo on the bus.
  2. Calibration — record the joint ranges of each arm, so leader and follower agree on every angle.
  3. Teleoperation — the follower mirrors the leader in real time, while the two cameras stream to the PC.

Teleoperation is not only a demo: it is how the robot learns. Every movement we make with the leader can be recorded as an example of the task.

Teaching the robot by demonstration

The goal was to remove the human: the robot should see the scene and pick up and place the object by itself, following the LeRobot imitation-learning workflow.

  1. Record demonstrations

    The task is performed many times through teleoperation. Each episode stores the two camera streams, the six joint positions and the actions sent to the follower, at 30 fps.

  2. Build the dataset

    Episodes are uploaded to the Hugging Face Hub and merged into one training set: 50 episodes and about 19,800 frames over 5 task variants.

  3. Check the data

    Before training, episodes are replayed with the LeRobot visualizer to catch bad recordings, wrong motions or desynchronized cameras.

  4. Train an ACT policy

    The policy is trained on a GPU in Google Colab and pushed back to the Hub, so the same model can be loaded on any machine.

  5. Run it on the robot

    The trained policy drives the follower directly from the camera images and joint states, with no leader arm connected.

Why ACT. Action Chunking with Transformers is the imitation-learning policy LeRobot suggests starting with. A ResNet backbone reads the camera images, a transformer combines them with the joint positions, and the network predicts a chunk of future actions at once instead of a single step. This makes the motion smoother and more robust, and good results are typically reached with about 50 demonstrations — the size of our dataset.

A training run on the merged dataset, as launched in Colab:

python src/lerobot/scripts/lerobot_train.py \
  --dataset.repo_id=SOARM1/SOARM101_merged \
  --policy.type=act \
  --policy.device=cuda \
  --output_dir=outputs/train/hf_act_record0 \
  --policy.repo_id=SOARM1/hf_act_recordpolicy0

In the team we also tuned the chunking, for example predicting 50 steps and executing 10 before re-planning, which gave smoother movements on the real arm.

Pick-and-place in the workcell

The workcell is deliberately simple: a cardboard enclosure with tape marking the work area, an object to grasp and a box to drop it into. Keeping the background and lighting consistent between recording and deployment matters, because the policy learns directly from pixels.

Two practical points of this workflow:

  • The setup is part of the model. Cameras, lighting and object positions should stay the same between recording and deployment.
  • Everything is reproducible. Datasets and trained policies live on the Hugging Face Hub, so anyone in the team can retrain or run a model on their own laptop.
