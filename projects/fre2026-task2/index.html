Marker detection and semantic Graph SLAM for an autonomous robot in a curved maize field

The Field Robot Event is an international competition for autonomous robots in real agricultural fields. In Task 2 the robot drives on its own through several curved rows of maize in three minutes, recognises the plants marked as “diseased” and reports each one: a public visual signal on the spot, plus a CSV log with the row number and the distance from the start of the row, within 10 cm.

I worked on the perception and localization side of the system: detecting the markers reliably outdoors and placing them on a map that does not drift.

The challenge

Diseased plants are simulated by rectangular markers made of yellow paper or jute, placed next to a plant. The task breaks down into three problems:

  1. Perception — find the markers under changing outdoor light, partly hidden by leaves.
  2. Localization & mapping — measure where each one is along a curved row, where wheel odometry alone drifts on loose soil.
  3. Real-time feedback — light up a signal while the marker is still inside the 40 cm zone.

The robot

Grasslammer is AIRLab’s four-wheeled field platform. For this task the relevant sensors were:

  • two OAK-D RGB-D cameras on the sides, one per row of plants;
  • two SICK single-plane LiDARs, front and rear;
  • a downward-facing ZED stereo camera;
  • a custom LED matrix on the back for the public signal.

Perception: from pixels to 3D markers

A ROS 2 detection node turns every camera frame into validated 3D marker positions.

  1. Calibrate on site

    Before each run, a region of interest is placed over a real marker (via ROS 2 dynamic parameters) and a service call stores its Hue–Saturation histogram, so the detector matches the light of that moment.

  2. Histogram back-projection

    Each frame is converted to HSV, which separates colour from brightness and copes better with shadows. Back-projecting the stored histogram gives a probability map of "marker-coloured" pixels.

  3. Clean the mask

    A threshold turns the map into a binary mask; morphological opening removes isolated noise and closing fills the gaps inside the marker.

  4. Check the shape

    Contours that are too small are dropped. The rest must pass aspect-ratio and extent tests, which separate rectangular markers from irregular blobs.

  5. Go to 3D

    The median of the valid aligned depth pixels and the camera intrinsics project the marker centre into 3D. Only markers inside the 40 cm lateral zone are passed on, in the robot frame.

Tracking and mapping

Raw detections are noisy: the same marker is seen in many frames, and reflections can look like markers. Before reaching the map they go through a multi-object tracker from the open-source orchards-semantic-slam library:

  • a cost matrix combines Euclidean distance (with a gating threshold) and semantic class (yellow vs jute), and the Hungarian algorithm finds the best assignment;
  • each track is smoothed by a Kalman filter and confirmed only after at least two hits.

Confirmed markers then enter a semantic factor graph solved with GTSAM / iSAM2. Odometry adds a new pose only after a minimum displacement, and each observation links a pose to a landmark through a range-bearing factor.

Anchoring both ends of the row. Only the distance from the row start matters, so a fresh graph is started at every row entry, which also discards the drift from the headland turns. A prior fixes the first pose at the origin; on exit, a second prior fixes the final position to the known row length. A last optimization then spreads the accumulated drift back over the whole trajectory.

Real-time signal

The LED matrix is driven by the tracker’s confirmation logic, not by single frames, so sensor noise does not cause false alarms. When a plant is confirmed, an arrow points left or right, towards the camera that saw it.

Results and lessons learned

The perception layer was robust against false positives in real outdoor conditions. The field showed two clear limits, which point to the next steps:

  • Calibration was too narrow. A histogram taken in one light and orientation sometimes missed markers seen at other angles. Combining several histograms, or widening the stored H–S range, should help.
  • Mapping missed the 10 cm target. The Graph SLAM pipeline worked end to end, but needs more tuning of the graph noise models in live tests to reach the required precision.
